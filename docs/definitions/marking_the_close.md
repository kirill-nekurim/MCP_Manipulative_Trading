# Marking the Close

**Document ID:** marking_the_close
**Source:** CFA Institute – Standard II(B) Market Manipulation
**URL:** https://www.cfainstitute.org/standards/professionals/code-ethics-standards/standards-of-practice-ii-b
**Document Type:** ethics_standard
**Language:** English

## Definition

Marking the Close is a form of transaction-based market manipulation in which a trader executes trades near the end of a trading session with the intent to influence the closing or settlement price of a financial instrument.

The key feature of this behavior is **intentional distortion of the price-setting mechanism** during a critical pricing window.

## Why Closing Prices Matter

Closing prices are widely used in financial markets and can affect:

* portfolio valuations
* fund net asset value (NAV)
* derivative settlements
* margin calculations
* performance benchmarks
* index and benchmark construction

Because of this importance, closing prices are a common target for manipulation.

## Core Mechanism

Marking the Close typically involves:

1. Establishing a position that benefits from a higher or lower closing price.
2. Executing aggressive trades near the end of the trading session.
3. Creating artificial price pressure during the settlement window.
4. Influencing the final closing price.
5. Benefiting from the resulting valuation change in existing positions.

## Example: Price Mark-Up

A trader holds a large long position in a security.

Near the market close, the trader buys additional shares aggressively.

This increased demand pushes the price upward during the settlement period.

The trader benefits from the higher closing price across their larger existing position.

## Example: Price Mark-Down

A trader holds a short position.

Near the close, the trader sells aggressively into the market.

The increased selling pressure pushes the closing price downward.

The trader benefits from the lower settlement price.

## Indicators of Potential Marking the Close

The following behaviors may indicate suspicious activity:

* unusually large trading activity near market close
* concentration of trading in a narrow time window
* aggressive order placement during settlement period
* imbalance in buy or sell-side trading
* price movement concentrated in the final trading period
* trading inconsistent with intraday behavior
* coordinated or repetitive patterns across accounts

These indicators alone do not prove manipulation but may trigger surveillance alerts.

## Transaction-Based Manipulation Characteristics

Marking the Close belongs to transaction-based manipulation, which includes:

* trades designed to mislead market participants about price or volume
* actions that distort the price formation mechanism
* artificial influence on observable market metrics

The key distinguishing factor is **intent to distort market perception**, not normal trading activity.

## Legitimate Trading vs Manipulation

Legitimate trading activity may resemble manipulation, especially in:

* illiquid markets
* volatile conditions
* end-of-day rebalancing
* institutional execution strategies

However, intent is critical:

* Legitimate trading seeks execution efficiency
* Marking the Close seeks price distortion

## Regulatory Perspective

Marking the Close violates market integrity principles under CFA Standard II(B), which prohibits:

> practices that distort prices or artificially inflate trading volume with intent to mislead market participants.

Regulators and compliance teams typically evaluate:

* timing of trades
* size relative to market volume
* aggressiveness of execution
* economic rationale of trades
* relationship to existing positions

## Example (Case Interpretation)

A trader holding a large position engages in aggressive trading near the close to influence settlement prices.

Even if trades are executed on an exchange and are technically valid, the activity violates market integrity rules if the intent is to move the closing price.

## Market Impact

Marking the Close can:

* distort price discovery
* create misleading valuation signals
* affect derivative payouts
* reduce investor confidence
* increase perceived market manipulation risk
